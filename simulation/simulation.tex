\section{Experimental Design}
In \autoref{chap:modelling} the EOSSAT-1 orbit was defined and it's un-synchronous orbit poses some unique challenges for the underactuated controller. Typical Earth Observation satellites operations involve a combination of nadir and sun pointing. In this section, these operations are defined and their implications for the underactuated controller are discussed. Given this information, choices and constraints for the experimental setup are discussed and metrics for evaluating the performance of the underactuated controller are defined. 

\subsection{Satellite Operations}

The two main important operations are important for Earth Observation satellites: imaging and sun pointing. During imaging, the satellite's camera is pointed towards the Earth to capture images usually towards nadir but can also be at other roll angles relative to nadir. During sun pointing the satellite's solar panels are pointed towards the Sun to maximize power generation. At 500km altitude, EOSSAT-1 will spend roughly half of its orbit in eclipse and the other half in sunlight. Typically the satellite will spend the sunlit portion of the orbit sun pointing with short bursts of nadir pointing for imaging. During eclipse any orientation is possible as no specific orientation is required, however a common method of operating is to point nadir. This is convienient for the experimental setup as it allows for a simple and repeatable scenario where the satellite is required to switch between nadir and sun pointing which will give an indication for the performance of the underactuated controller in a realistic scenario. 


\subsection{Nadir and Sun Pointing Frame Definitions}
Given the way that EOSSAT-1's body frame axes are defined in \autoref{subsec:reference_frames}, nadir pointing becomes the orbit (aka. LVLH) frame $F_O$. For the purposes of this simulation, an additional reference frame is defined: the sun pointing frame $F_S$. The sun pointing frame is defined such that the $x$-axis points anti-parallel to the body-sun vector $x_S = -\vec{r}_{SB}$. The nadir pointing frame is therefore fully constrained where as the sun pointing frame is not fully constrained; the z-axis can be chosen to be any vector perpendicular to the $x$-axis. Typically the $z$-axis is chosen to be the shortest vector to the nadir vector, however it will be shown that this choice has important implications given the constraints of the underactuated system. The standard sun triad can be calculated as follows:

\begin{align*}
    [T]^{SI}  &= \begin{bmatrix}
        \hat{x}_S \
        \hat{y}_S \
        \hat{z}_S
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \hat{x}_S &= -\vec{r}_{SB} \\
    \hat{z}_O &= \frac{\vec{r}_{OB}}{\|\vec{r}_{OB}\|} \\
    \hat{y}_S &= \hat{z}_O \times \hat{x}_S \\
    \hat{z}_S &= \hat{x}_S \times \hat{y}_S 
\end{align*}

\subsection{Eclipse Transition Planning}

To mitigate the above mentioned issues with the underactuated controller, instead of defining the sun pointing frame with x-axis directly pointed towards the sun and z-axis defined as the shortest vector to nadir, the z-axis is defined as pointing anti-parallel to the velocity vector (to avoid the camera payload pointing directly into the high velocity atmospheric air flow) and the x-axis is defined as the shortest vector to the sun. This avoids the need for large yaw rotations and simply requires a $180\degree$ roll followed by a pitch manoeuvre.

Therefore, a slow yaw rotation is executed over the course of the sunlit portion of the orbit.
