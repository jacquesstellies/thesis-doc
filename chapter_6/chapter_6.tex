\chapter{Magnetorquer Controller}

As mentioned in \autoref{chap:modelling}, magnetorquers are the only mechanism for dumping momentum build up on a satellite with reaction wheels. Furthermore, the FNDO-BS controller derived in \autoref{chap:underactuated_controller} requires that the angular momentum be zero, i.e., $\vec{h}=0$. Finally due to the lack of authority of said controller on the underactuated axis near the equilibrium, small steady-state errors can build over time during manoeuvres on the dead axis.  In this chapter, a magnetorquer controller is presented which must address these issues. A simple allocation scheme that achieves both objectives, and demonstrates its performance in simulation.

\section{Torque Generation}
A simple proportional controller is used to generate a torque that is opposite to the angular momentum of the system. Reiterating \autoref{eq:magt_torque} given in \autoref{chap:modelling}, torque generated by the magnetorquers $\vec{\tau}_m$ is given by \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}:

% is this body frame?
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{\tau}_m = \vec{m} \times \vec{B}
    \label{eq:magt_torque_2}
\end{equation*}

where $\vec{m}$ is the magnetic moment of the magnetorquers and $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field strength at the satellite's location in body coordinates. \autoref{eq:magt_torque} is not invertible, however with some simplifying assumptions a magnetic moment $m$ can be determined. Assuming that the commanded torque is always perpendicular to the local field - $\vec{\tau}_m \perp \vec{B}$ for every commanded dipole. This means the set of torques reachable at any instant is limited to the plane normal to the local field rather than the whole of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Writing $\hat{\vec{B}} = \vec{B}/\|\vec{B}\|$, the minimum-norm dipole realising a commanded torque $\vec{\tau}_c$ is \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}

\begin{equation}
    \vec{m} = \frac{\vec{B} \times \vec{\tau}_c}{\|\vec{B}\|^2}
    \label{eq:magt_alloc}
\end{equation}

and the torque actually delivered is the orthogonal projection of the demand onto that plane,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_m = \vec{m} \times \vec{B} = \left(\vec{I}_3 - \hat{\vec{B}}\hat{\vec{B}}^T\right)\vec{\tau}_c \triangleq \vec{P}_B\,\vec{\tau}_c
    \label{eq:magt_projection}
\end{equation}
 A torque demand parallel to the instantaneous field direction produces a commanded dipole that is zero. This is can be observed mathematically by noting that the eigenvalues of $\vec{P}_B$ are ($1, 1, 0$) which means it has rank 2.
\section{Momentum Dumping}
\label{subsec:null_space_parking}
The conventional momentum dumping control law sets the demand proportional to the total angular momentum, 
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_c = -k\,\vec{\Delta h}, \ k > 0
    \label{eq:mag_dump_law}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{\Delta h} = \vec{h} - \vec{h}_d$ is the difference between the current and desired angular momentum.

Substituting $\vec{\tau}_c$ into \autoref{eq:magt_projection}, only the component of $\vec{h}$ perpendicular to the field is removed \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997},
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vec{h}} = -k\,\vec{P}_B\,\vec{\Delta h} = -k\left(\vec{h} - \hat{\vec{B}}(\hat{\vec{B}}^T\vec{\Delta h})\right)
    \label{eq:dump_continuous}
\end{equation}
Because the law is implemented at a sample period $\Delta t$ with a zero-order hold, the discrete update is
\begin{equation}
    \vec{h}_{k+1} = (1 - k\Delta t)\,\vec{h}_k + k\Delta t\,\hat{\vec{B}}_k(\hat{\vec{B}}_k^T\vec{h}_k)
    \label{eq:dump_discrete}
\end{equation}
Note that $k$ carries units of $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ with $\vec{\tau}_c = -k\vec{h}$ the gain \emph{is} the commanded decay rate, not a dimensionless tuning constant. It is instructive to consider the deadbeat control law case. Setting $k\Delta t = 1$ gives the deadbeat case
\begin{equation}
    \vec{h}_{k+1} = \hat{\vec{B}}_k(\hat{\vec{B}}_k^T\vec{h}_k)
\end{equation}
After a single sample the entire surviving momentum lies entirely along $\hat{\vec{B}}_k$, that is, inside the null space of $\vec{P}_B$. On the next cycle the demand is parallel to the field, the commanded dipole is zero, and the controller stalls. The momentum has been driven into the one direction the actuator cannot address. Intuitively, a it removes all the momentum it can actuate, and what it leaves behind is what cannot. However total momentum can be removed over the span of orbits as $\hat{\vec{B}}$ rotates relative to $\vec{h}$. 

% For a near-circular orbit this rotation occurs at approximately twice the orbital rate, $\omega_B \approx 2n$, which for the 500\,km, $i \approx 90^\circ$ orbit used throughout this work is $\omega_B \approx 2.2\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Over one sample the field direction moves by $\varepsilon = \omega_B\Delta t$, so the momentum left pointing at the previous field direction is no longer quite aligned with the new one and a fraction $\sin\varepsilon$ becomes visible again. Under deadbeat action all of it is removed, leaving
% \begin{equation}
%     \|\vec{h}_{k+1}\| = \|\vec{h}_k\|\cos\varepsilon \approx \|\vec{h}_k\|\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^2\right)
%     \label{eq:parking_leak}
% \end{equation}
% The leak is \emph{quadratic} in the sweep angle. For $\Delta t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ this gives a decay rate of $2.4\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, a time constant of roughly $4.8$ days. A simulation of the law at $k = 1$, $\Delta t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ reproduces this: the residual momentum settles at $0.00^\circ$ from the field direction and decays with a fitted time constant of $4.6$ days, effectively frozen on any timescale of operational interest.

% Reducing the gain so that $k\Delta t \ll 1$ prevents the collapse, since the momentum is no longer driven fully into the null space of $\vec{P}_B$ on each sample. 

Reducing the gain so that $k\Delta t \ll 1$ prevents the residual momentum from
being driven onto $\hat{\vec{B}}$: each sample removes only a fraction $k\Delta t$
of the perpendicular component, so the field's rotation keeps a non-zero
actuatable component available. 
No analytical procedure for selecting $k$ is available
\cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}: the closed loop is time-varying, since
$\vec{B}$ varies in direction and magnitude around the orbit, and the allocation
\eqref{eq:magt_alloc} delivers only the projection $\vec{P}_B\vec{\tau}_c$ of the
demanded torque rather than $\vec{\tau}_c$ itself, so $k$ does not set the decay
rate directly. As in \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}, $k$ is therefore
selected by simulation.
\subsection{Gain Sweep}

The sweep is a free-decay test: the dumping law is integrated from an initial momentum along the orbit's field history with no disturbance torques. Since $\vec{\tau}_m = -k\vec{P}_B\vec{h}$ is frame-independent and reaction wheels cannot alter $\vec{h}$, the result is independent of the spacecraft attitude. In operation the residual settles at a floor set by the disturbance torques rather than decaying indefinitely. \autoref{tab:dump_gain_sweep} and \autoref{fig:dump_gain_sweep} show $\|\vec{h}\|$ over five orbits for a sweep of $k$, with an initial momentum of $\|\vec{h}_0\| = 0.226\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$, directed equally along the three body axes and limited to $m_{max} = 17\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$. This corresponds to the total momentum accumulated over one orbit without dumping, in a full closed-loop simulation of the nominal day/night profile under the disturbance model of \autoref{chap:modelling}. Because the dumping law is linear in $\vec{h}$ and independent of the spacecraft inertia, the normalised decay does not depend on $\vec{J}$, nor on $\|\vec{h}_0\|$ while the dipole limit is not reached. Performance is non-monotonic in $k$: at high gain the residual lies along $\hat{\vec{B}}$ and barely decays, and at low gain dumping is simply too slow. The best result, $k = 3\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, is of the same order as the field rotation rate $\omega_B \approx 2n$. It is also the only gain that drives the residual below numerical precision within the window.

\subsection{Dipole Saturation}
Because \autoref{eq:magt_alloc} can demand an arbitrarily large dipole as $\|\vec{B}\|$ falls or the demand grows, the commanded moment must be limited to the physically available $m_{max}$. Saturating each body component independently, $m_i \leftarrow \mathrm{sat}(m_i, m_{max})$, rotates $\vec{m}$ away from the direction $\vec{B} \times \vec{\tau}_c$, so the delivered torque $\vec{m} \times \vec{B}$ is no longer parallel to the momentum being removed and can add momentum on individual axes. Scaling the vector instead,
\begin{equation}
    \vec{m} \leftarrow \vec{m}\,\min\!\left(1, \frac{m_{max}}{\|\vec{m}\|}\right)
    \label{eq:dipole_scaling}
\end{equation}
preserves the torque direction and reduces only its magnitude, which is the correct behaviour under authority limits.

\subsection{The Unactuated Axis Is the Binding Constraint}
\label{subsec:dead_axis_constraint}
The preceding analysis concerns $\|\vec{h}\|$ as a whole, but for the underactuated spacecraft the quantity that actually determines pointing performance is the component of $\vec{h}$ along the unactuated axis. Reaction wheel torques are internal and cannot change $\vec{h}$; they only redistribute it between body and wheels. With the $z$-axis wheel failed, $h_{wz} = \dot{h}_{wz} = 0$, so
\begin{equation}
    J_z\,\omega_z = h_z - h_{wz} = h_z
    \label{eq:dead_axis_rate}
\end{equation}
and the yaw rate is fixed entirely by the projection of the total momentum onto the current body $z$ axis. Since only external torques change $\vec{h}$, the yaw angle is the double integral of the unactuated axis disturbance and magnetic torques, with no restoring term from the wheels of any kind,
\begin{equation}
    \psi(t) = \frac{1}{J_z}\int_0^t h_z(t')\,dt', \qquad \ddot{\psi} = \frac{\tau_{dist,z} + \tau_{m,z}}{J_z}
    \label{eq:yaw_double_integral}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{dist,z}$ is the environmental disturbance torque and $\tau_{m,z} = \vec{\tau}_m\cdot\hat{\vec{e}}_z$ is the magnetorquer torque on the dead axis. The magnetorquer is therefore the only actuator with any authority over $\psi$.

\autoref{fig:inertial_hold_magt} shows a $6000\,\mathrm{s}$ inertial hold with the $z$-axis wheel failed and the momentum dumping law of \autoref{eq:mag_dump_law} active at $k = 3\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The actuated roll and pitch axes are held to within $10^{-4}\,^\circ$, and the dumping keeps $\|\vec{h}\|$ below $0.036\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$ with a peak dipole of $3.1\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$. Yaw is nevertheless left uncontrolled, peaking at $56.0^\circ$ at $t \approx 5424\,\mathrm{s}$. Integrating the unactuated axis disturbance and magnetic torques twice reproduces the logged yaw to within $0.1^\circ$ RMS, confirming \autoref{eq:yaw_double_integral}. For comparison, the disturbance torque alone integrates to $276^\circ$ over the same run, so the dumping does slow the drift. However, it acts on $\vec{h}$ isotropically, at an orbit-scale rate set by $k$, and applies no feedback on $\psi$ itself, so the unactuated axis error it leaves behind is still tens of degrees.

\subsection{Split Allocation}
\label{subsec:split_allocation}
The observations above motivate treating the magnetorquer not as a momentum dumper but as the dedicated actuator on that axis. Since the unactuated axis dynamics are the double integrator of \autoref{eq:yaw_double_integral}, a proportional-derivative law is appropriate, and its gains can be chosen by pole placement.

Let $\psi_e$ denote the yaw error relative to the reference, so that $\dot{\psi}_e = \omega_z - \omega_{d,z}$, where $\omega_{d,z}$ is the desired angular rate about the dead axis. Assuming the reference acceleration $\dot{\omega}_{d,z}$ is negligible, \autoref{eq:yaw_double_integral} gives the error dynamics
\begin{equation}
    J_z\ddot{\psi}_e = \tau_{dist,z} + \tau_{m,z}
    \label{eq:yaw_error_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{m,z}$ is the control torque applied by the magnetorquer on the z axis.
Using this a PD law control law can be defined to regulate the yaw error similarly to the quaternion feedback controller defined in \autoref{chap:modelling}
% The desired closed loop is a damped second-order system driven by the disturbance,
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{m,z} = - J_z k_P\psi_e - J_z k_D\dot{\psi}_e
    \label{eq:yaw_pd_ideal}
\end{equation}

It is standard to choose $k_P = \omega_n^2$ and $k_D = 2\zeta\omega_n$ where $\omega_n$ is the natural frequency and $\zeta$ the damping ratio so that the closed loop is a damped second-order system.


which when substituted into \autoref{eq:yaw_error_dynamics} gives the closed-loop dynamics
\begin{equation}
      \ddot{\psi}_e + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{\psi}_e + \omega_n^2\psi_e = \frac{\tau_{dist,z}}{J_z}
    \label{eq:yaw_closed_loop}
\end{equation}

The vector part of the body-frame error quaternion for a rotation $\psi_e$ about $\hat{\vec{e}}_z$ is $q_{e,z} = \sin(\psi_e/2)$, so for small errors $\psi_e \approx -2q_{e,z}$ and substituting into \autoref{eq:yaw_pd_ideal} gives the final unactuated axis control law
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{m,z} = -2\zeta\omega_n - 2J_z\omega_n^2\,q_{e,z}
    \label{eq:dead_axis_pd}
\end{equation}

Setting the derivatives in \autoref{eq:yaw_closed_loop} to zero, a slowly varying disturbance leaves a steady-state error of
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{e,ss} \approx \frac{\tau_{dist,z}}{J_z\,\omega_n^2}
    \label{eq:yaw_ss_error}
\end{equation}
The bandwidth $\omega_n$ must lie well above the orbital rate so that the unactuated axis disturbance is rejected, and well below $1/\Delta t$. 

The remaining two axes retain the slow dumping law of \autoref{subsec:null_space_parking} applied to $\vec{h}$ with its unactuated axis component removed. However, combining the momentum dumping and unactuated axis PD control law requires care. Naively summing them and passing the result through \autoref{eq:magt_alloc} does not decouple them, because the dumping demand on the actuated axes projects a spurious torque onto the dead axis. In simulation this torque was of order $3\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, more than ten times the $1.9\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ disturbance the unactuated axis loop is attempting to reject, so the yaw error settled where the PD command balanced the leakage rather than the disturbance, degrading the achieved pointing by roughly two orders of magnitude.

Exact decoupling follows from observing that the unactuated axis torque is
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_m\cdot\hat{\vec{e}}_z = (\vec{m}\times\vec{B})\cdot\hat{\vec{e}}_z = \vec{m}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\hat{\vec{e}}_z) \triangleq \vec{m}\cdot\vec{u}
    \label{eq:dead_axis_direction}
\end{equation}
so that only the component of $\vec{m}$ along $\vec{u} = \vec{B}\times\hat{\vec{e}}_z$ produces any unactuated axis torque, and every dipole perpendicular to $\vec{u}$ produces none. Splitting the commanded dipole on $\vec{u}$ therefore separates the two objectives,
\begin{align}
    \vec{m}_{\psi} &= \frac{\tau_{m,z}}{\|\vec{u}\|^2}\,\vec{u} \label{eq:m_yaw}\\
    \vec{m}_{dump} &= \frac{\vec{B}\times\vec{\tau}_{c,\perp}}{\|\vec{B}\|^2} - \frac{(\vec{B}\times\vec{\tau}_{c,\perp})\cdot\vec{u}}{\|\vec{B}\|^2\,\|\vec{u}\|^2}\,\vec{u} \label{eq:m_dump}\\
    \vec{m} &= \vec{m}_{\psi} + \vec{m}_{dump} \label{eq:m_total}
\end{align}
where $\vec{\tau}_{c,\perp}$ is the dumping demand on the actuated axes; the full derivation is given in \autoref{appen:split_allocation}. Since $\vec{m}_{\psi} \parallel \vec{u}$ and $\vec{m}_{dump} \perp \vec{u}$ by construction, the two are orthogonal and saturation with the dead axis given priority has the closed form
\begin{equation}
    \vec{m}_{dump} \leftarrow \vec{m}_{dump}\min\!\left(1, \frac{\sqrt{m_{max}^2 - \|\vec{m}_{\psi}\|^2}}{\|\vec{m}_{dump}\|}\right)
    \label{eq:priority_saturation}
\end{equation}
with $\vec{m}_{\psi}$ itself scaled back only if it alone exceeds $m_{max}$. In the degenerate case $\vec{B} \parallel \hat{\vec{e}}_z$ the vector $\vec{u}$ vanishes and no unactuated axis authority exists for that cycle; the yaw term is then simply suppressed. For a nadir-pointing spacecraft this occurs where the field is radial, that is, near the magnetic poles. In the orbit studied the minimum angle between $\vec{B}$ and the dead axis was $1.5^\circ$ and the available unactuated axis torque never fell below a factor of $59$ above the disturbance, so these windows proved to be of no practical consequence.

\subsection{Simulation Results}
\autoref{tab:split_allocation_results} and \autoref{fig:split_allocation_hold} compare the isotropic dumping law in conjunction with the BS-FNDO controller against the split allocation over a $6000\,\mathrm{s}$ inertial hold in full closed-loop simulation, with the $z$-axis wheel failed. The magnetorquer $m_{max} = 17\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$ and $\zeta = 0.707$. The isotropic case is the run of \autoref{fig:inertial_hold_magt}. In every case the actuated roll and pitch errors remain below $3\times10^{-4}\,^\circ$.

The split allocation reduces the peak unactuated-axis error to $\leq0.12\degree$ which is $2$ orders of magnitude relative to isotropic dumping. The error tracks $\tau_{dist,z}(t)/(J_z\omega_n^2)$ throughout the run rather than settling to a constant, and its peak agrees with the prediction of \autoref{eq:yaw_ss_error} to within $1\%$. Doubling $\omega_n$ reduces the error fourfold, as the $1/\omega_n^2$ dependence requires. At $\omega_n = 0.005\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, only about four times the orbital rate, the response lags the disturbance noticeably, although the peak is still predicted closely.

The peak dipole occurs in a brief window near $t \approx 5960\,\mathrm{s}$, where the dead-axis authority $\|\vec{B}\times\hat{\vec{e}}_z\|$ is small and the required dipole grows accordingly. At $\omega_n = 0.005\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ the limit is reached for $0.1\%$ of the run. Away from such windows the demand is modest: the median dipole is $1.2\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$ and the $99$th percentile is between $5.6$ and $7.2\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$ across the three bandwidths.

A separate result emerged when the same allocation was exercised during a commanded yaw manoeuvre rather than a hold. Because the wheels cannot produce unactuated axis torque, the box manoeuvre steers yaw by pumping $h_z$ through the gyroscopic term $-(\vec\omega\times\vec{h})_z$, which is precisely the quantity the rate feedback in \autoref{eq:dead_axis_pd} damps. It was therefore anticipated that the unactuated axis loop would oppose the manoeuvre and that detuning it during the manoeuvre would improve tracking. \autoref{tab:manoeuvre_gain} shows the opposite.

The magnetorquer is not competing with the wheels for yaw control but supplying the majority of it. The in-plane momentum available to the manoeuvre was within $15\%$ across these cases, so the difference is attributable to the unactuated axis loop rather than to the dumping law depriving the manoeuvre of the momentum it requires.

\subsection{Limitations}
Three limitations of the above should be noted. The gain sweep of \autoref{tab:dump_gain_sweep} is a free-decay replay of the dumping law along the logged field history, without disturbances or the attitude controller. The hold results of \autoref{tab:split_allocation_results} and the manoeuvre results of \autoref{tab:manoeuvre_gain} are from full closed-loop simulation, but each represents a single trajectory at one orbital phase, so the quoted figures should be read as indicative rather than as general performance bounds. Finally, the loop of \autoref{eq:dead_axis_pd} is normalised in gain with respect to inertia but not in authority: the dipole required scales as $J_z\omega_n^2\psi/(\|\vec{B}\|\sin\angle(\vec{B},\hat{\vec{e}}_z))$, so a spacecraft of substantially larger moment of inertia will require proportionally more dipole to sustain the same bandwidth, and the $15\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$ used here may not be sufficient.
