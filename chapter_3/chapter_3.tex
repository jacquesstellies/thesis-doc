\chapter{Modelling}
In this chapter the framework is built to represent the underactuated spacecraft problem. First the attitude control of a satellite is described, including the kinematics and dynamics. Then the orbit and environment are described, including the dominant disturbances acting on a satellite in low earth orbit. Finally the reaction wheel dynamics are described, with relevant fault mechanisms investigated.
A note on notation in this thesis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vectors are denoted by bold lowercase letters, e.g.\ $\vec{x}$.
    \item Matrices are denoted by bold uppercase letters, e.g.\ $\vec{A}$.
    \item Scalars are denoted by lowercase letters, e.g.\ $a$.
\end{itemize}
It is also worth defining the following notation for sub and superscripts of vectors and matrices:

\begin{itemize}
    \item subscripts denote points, e.g.\ $\vec{r}_{AB}$ is the position vector of point from point B to point A (A relative to B).
    \item superscripts denote frames, e.g.\ $\vec{v}^I_B$ is the velocity vector of point B relative to frame inertial frame I and $\vec{\omega}^{BI}$ is the angular velocity of frame B relative to I.
    \item coordinates are denoted by superscript outside of brackets e.g.\ [$\vec{r}_{AB}]^I$ represents the position vector of point A relative to B expressed in coordinates of frame I.
\end{itemize}
These notations may be ommitted when context makes one aspect or another clear. Other lowercase and upper case sub and superscripts may be used to denote other operators, quantities etc. which should be clear given context.


\section{Attitude Control of a Satellite}
\subsection{Reference Frames and Attitude Representations}
Since the goal is to control the attitude of a satellite, an appropriate representation should be used. Any orientation can be representead as a rotation from one frame to another. Typical reference frames would be earth centered inertial (ECI), earth-centered-earth-fixed (ECEF), orbit or body-fixed. A list of reference frames used with their abbreviation is given as:

\vspace{1cm}

The satellite's orientation can be represented intuitively using euler angles yaw $\psi$, pitch $\theta$ and roll $\phi$, also known as the aerospace sequence. While these are intuitive, they suffer from singularities when trying to represent certain rotations. The direction cosine matrix (DCM) is the most fundamental representation of a rotation, since it is the rotation operator itself rather than a parameterization of it — it acts directly on a vector and is free of the singularities and sign ambiguities present in other representations. It can be used to transform a vector from frame A to B using the following relation 
\begin{equation}
    [\vec{v}]^B = [\vec{C}]^{BA}[\vec{v}]^A
\end{equation}

where $[\vec{C}]^{BA} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is the DCM from frame A to B and is made up of the orthonormal basis vectors of frame B expressed in frame A: $[\vec{C}]^{BA} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{\bar{a}}_1^B & \vec{\bar{a}}_2^B & \vec{\bar{a}}_3^B\end{bmatrix}$.

This matrix can be described by the euler 3-2-1 rotation sequence as:

\begin{align}
    % \begin{split}
    [\vec{C}]^{BA} &= \begin{bmatrix}
        c_{11} & c_{12} & c_{13} \\
        c_{21} & c_{22} & c_{23} \\
        c_{31} & c_{32} & c_{33}
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    &= \begin{bmatrix}
        C \psi C \theta &  S\psi C \theta & - S \theta \\
        - S \psi C \phi + C \psi S \theta S \phi & C \psi C \phi +  S\psi S \theta S \phi & C \theta S \phi \\
        C \psi S \theta C \phi + S \psi S \phi & S \psi S \theta C \phi - C \psi S \phi & C \theta C \phi
    \end{bmatrix} 
\end{align}

The given problem requires computing attitude kinematics over time, and while DCMs can be used for this purpose, it is cumbersome requiring computationaly expensive trignometric functions. Instead quaternions, $\vec{q} = [q_{x} \ q_{y} \ q_{z} \ q_{w}]^T = [\vec{q}_v, q_w]^T$, are used to represent the kinematic differential equations. Quaternions represent a 4 dimensional extension of complex numbers. In order to represent rotations we limit ourselves to the unit quaternion which satisfies $q_{x}^2 + q_{y}^2 + q_{z}^2 + q_{w}^2 = 1$.

Any sequence of rotations can be represented as a single rotation by angle $\Phi$ about some vector $\vec{e}$. The rotation quaternion can be represented in these terms as:

% \begin{align}
$$
    q_{x}=e_{x}\sin(\Phi), \
    q_{y}=e_{y}\sin(\Phi), \
    q_{z}=e_{z}\sin(\Phi), \
    q_{w}=cos(\Phi)
$$
% \end{align}

The quaternion can be used to compute the DCM as follows:

\begin{align}
    \vec{C} = \begin{bmatrix}
        q_{x}^2 -q_{y}^2 - q_{z}^2 + q_{w}^2 & 2(q_{x}q_{y} - q_{z}q_{w}) & 2(q_{x}q_{z} + q_{y}q_{w}) \\
        2(q_{x}q_{y} + q_{z}q_{w}) & -q_{x}^2 + q_{y}^2 - q_{z}^2 + q_{w}^2 & 2(q_{y}q_{z} - q_{x}q_{w}) \\
        2(q_{x}q_{z} - q_{y}q_{w}) & 2(q_{y}q_{z} + q_{x}q_{w}) & -q_{x}^2 - q_{y}^2 + q_{z}^2 + q_{w}^2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

Quaternion multiplication is defined according to the hamiltonian (right-handed) convention as follows:
\begin{align}
    \vec{p} \otimes \vec{q} = \begin{bmatrix}
        p_w \vec{q}_v + q_w \vec{p}_v + \vec{p}_v \times \vec{q}_v \\
        p_w q_w - \vec{p}_v \cdot \vec{q}_v \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where $\vec{p} = [\vec{p}_v, p_w]^T$ and $\vec{q} = [\vec{q}_v, q_w]^T$ are quaternions, made up of their vector and scalar parts respectively. The conjugate of a quaternion is defined as $\vec{q}^* = [-\vec{q}_v, q_w]^T$ and the inverse of a quaternion is defined as $\vec{q}^{-1} = \frac{\vec{q}^*}{||\vec{q}||^2}$.

\subsubsection{Rotation Operators}
There are a 2 ways of using rotation operators, either active or passive. An active rotation rotates a vector in a fixed frame, while a passive rotation rotates the frame itself. The active rotation is the conjugate of the passive rotation $\vec{q}_{active} = \vec{q}^{*}_{passive}$. In this thesis the passive rotation convention is used.

Quaternions can be used to rotate a vector $\vec{v}$ from frame A to frame B using the following relation:

\begin{equation}
    [\vec{v}]^{B} = [\vec{q}]^{BA} \otimes [\vec{v}]^{A} \otimes [\vec{q}^*]^{BA}
\end{equation}

Since it is required to determine the attitude error for the controller, another useful relation is the quaternion error. The error quaternion can be calculated as:

\begin{align}
    \vec{q}_e = [\vec{q}]^{BR} =  \vec{q}_d^{-1} \otimes \vec{q} \otimes \\
\end{align}

\subsection{Attitude Kinematics}
To describe the kinematics, the inertial angular velocity of the spacecraft $\vec{\omega} = \begin{bmatrix}\omega_x \ \omega_y \ \omega_z \end{bmatrix}$ now can be expressed in terms of quaternions in the following manner:


$$\begin{bmatrix} \omega_x \\ \omega_y \\ \omega_z \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 2 
\begin{bmatrix} q_{w} & q_{z} & -q_{y} & -q_{x} 
            \\ -q_{z} & -q_{w} & q_{x} & -q_{y} 
            \\ q_{y} & -q_{x} & q_{w} & -q_{z}
            \\  q_{x} & q_{y} & q_{z} & q_{w}
\end{bmatrix} 
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{q}_{x} \\ \dot{q}_{y} \\ \dot{q}_{z} \\ \dot{q}_{w} \end{bmatrix}$$

and can be rearranged to form

\begin{align}
    \vec{\dot{q}}
=\frac{1}{2}
\begin{bmatrix}
q_{w} & -q_{z} & q_{y} \\ 
q_{z} & q_{w} & -q_{x} \\ 
-q_{y} & q_{x} & q_{w} \\ 
-q_{x} & -q_{y} & -q_{z} \\
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\omega_x \\ \omega_y \\ \omega_z \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
&= \frac{1}{2} \vec{q} \otimes \begin{bmatrix}\vec{\omega} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

we are interested in the error dynamics which can be expressed as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:q_error_kinematics}
    \vec{\dot{q}}_e = \frac{1}{2} \vec{q}_e \otimes \begin{bmatrix}\vec{\omega}_e \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{\omega}_e = \vec{\omega} - [\vec{C}]^{BR}\vec{\omega}_d$ is the angular velocity error of the satellite. It's time derivative can be expressed as \cite{nadafiRobustBacksteppingAttitude2021}: 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega_error}
    \vec{\dot{\omega}}_e = \vec{\dot{\omega}} - [\vec{C}]^{BR}\vec{\dot{\omega}}_d + \vec{\omega}_e \times [\vec{C}]^{BR}\vec{\omega}_d
\end{equation}


\subsection{Attitude Dynamics}

For the purposes of this thesis as a rigid body. The angular acceleration $\vec{\dot{\omega}}$ of a rigid body relative to the inertial frame with mass moment of inertia $\vec{J}$ can be modelled using the Newton-Euler equation:

\begin{equation}
\vec{J}\vec{\dot{\omega}} = \sum{\vec{\tau_{ext}}} -\vec{H}\times\vec{\omega}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{J}$ is the satellite's mass moment of inertia, $\vec{\tau_{ext}}$ is any external torque applied and $\vec{h}$ is the total angular momentum of the satellite. Since we are focusing on reaction wheel control system

\begin{equation} \label{eq:sat_dynamics}
\vec{J}\vec{\dot{\omega}} = \vec{\tau}_d -\vec{\omega}^\times(\vec{J}\vec{\omega}-\vec{h}_w)-\dot{\vec{h}}_w + \vec{\tau}_{mag}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{\tau}_d$ represents the external disturbances applied, $\vec{h}_w$ represents the total reaction wheels angular momentum vector, and $\vec{\tau}_{mag}$ represents the magnetorquer applied torque. $\vec{\omega}^\times$ represents the skew symmetric matrix of the body angular velocity vector.

\subsection{Underactuated Case}
\label{subsec:underactuated}
For the underactuated case, we will assume that a reaction wheel has failed on the z-axis and is no longer able to provide torque about its axis $h_{wz} = \dot{h}_{wz} = 0$. This means that the satellite is now underactuated and cannot control its attitude about that axis. A key assumption here for a controllablitity, mentioned in the previous chapter, is that the spacecraft total angular momentum is zero. This condition must be achieved via some torque, in this case magnetorquers. Thus according to \ref{eq:q_error_kinematics}, \ref{eq:omega_error}, \ref{eq:sat_dynamics} the kinematics of the underactuated case can be expressed as \cite{nadafiRobustBacksteppingAttitude2021}:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\dot{q}}_{ve} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} q_{ew} & -q_{ez} \\ 
q_{ez} & q_{ew} \\
-q_{ey} & q_{ex} \\
\end{bmatrix} \vec{\omega}_e^r = \vec{A}(\vec{q_{ve}}) \vec{\omega}_e^r
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\dot{\omega}}_e^r = - \vec{C}^r \vec{\dot{\omega}}_d^r + \{ c_{31} \omega_{dx} + c_{32} \omega_{dy} \} [\omega_{ey} \ -\omega_{ex}]^T + \vec{J}^{-1}(\vec{\tau}_d -\dot{\vec{h}}_w + \vec{\tau}_{mag})
\end{equation}

where each vector in the form of $ \vec{O}^r \in R^{2 \times 1} $ is equal
to  $ [Ox Oy]^T$ based on components of vector $\vec{O} \in R^3$, and
each matrix in the form of $\vec{P}^r \in R^{2 \times 2}$ is equal to
$\begin{bmatrix} P_{xx} & P_{xy} \\ P_{yx} & P_{yy} \end{bmatrix}$
based on elements of matrix $\vec{P} \in R^{3 \times 3}$. Similar expressions can be derived for failures in other axes.
\section{Orbit and Environment}
A common orbit used for observation satellites is a sun-synchronous low earth orbit (LEO). This orbit allows the satellite to pass over the same part of the earth at the same local solar time. This is useful for imaging and observation as the lighting conditions will be similar for each pass. A typical altitude for a sun-synchronous LEO is between 400 and 800 km. 

Disturbance torques acting on a satellite in LEO are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aerodynamic drag torque
    \item Gravity gradient torque
    \item Solar radiation pressure torque
    \item Magnetic Dipole torque
\end{itemize} 

\subsection{Aerodynamic Torque}
Aerodynamic drag forces arise from the interaction of the satellite with the residual atmosphere present in LEO. Although the drag force is complex and time-varying due to atmospheric density fluctuations, it can be approximated under simplifying assumptions \cite{auretDesignAerodynamicAttitude2011}. For a near-circular orbit, the angle $x_{o,east}$ between the co-rotating atmosphere and the orbital path is given by

\begin{equation}
    x_{o,east} = \begin{cases}
        \pi - \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{d\lambda/dt}{d\phi/dt}\right) & \text{for } \dfrac{d\phi}{dt} < 0 \\[8pt]
        \dfrac{\pi}{2} & \text{for } \dfrac{d\phi}{dt} = 0 \\[8pt]
        -\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{d\lambda/dt}{d\phi/dt}\right) & \text{for } \dfrac{d\phi}{dt} > 0
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda$ is the satellite latitude and $\phi$ is the longitude. The local atmospheric velocity vector expressed in the satellite body coordinate (SBC) frame is then

\begin{equation}
    \vec{v}^B_A = \vec{C}^{BO}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -\|\vec{v}_o\| + \omega^{EI} \|\vec{r}_{BE}\| \cos(\lambda)\cos(x_{o,east}) \\
        -\omega^{EI} \|\vec{r}_{BE}\| \cos(\lambda)\sin(x_{o,east}) \\
        0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\|\vec{v}_o\|$ is the orbital speed, $\omega^{EI}$ is the Earth's rotation rate, and $\vec{r}_{BE}$ is the orbital radius. The atmospheric density $\rho$ required to evaluate the drag torque can be estimated with an exponential model

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \rho_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{h - h_0}{H}\right)
\end{equation}

where $h$ is the orbit altitude, $h_0$ is a reference base altitude, $H$ is the atmospheric scale height, and $\rho_0$ is the nominal atmospheric density at $h_0$. The aerodynamic disturbance torque contribution of a given satellite surface is then \cite{auretDesignAerodynamicAttitude2011}

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_{\text{aero}} = \rho \|\vec{v}^B_A\|^2 A_p \left[\sigma_t\left(\vec{r}_{PB} \times \bar{\vec{v}}^B_A\right) + \left(\sigma_n \frac{v_b}{\|\vec{v}^B_A\|} + (2 - \sigma_n - \sigma_t)\cos\alpha\right)\!\left(\vec{r}_{PB} \times \bar{\vec{n}}\right)\right]
    \label{eq:aero_torque}
\end{equation}

where $\cos\alpha = \bar{\vec{v}}^B_A \cdot \bar{\vec{n}}$ is the cosine of the incidence angle of the atmospheric velocity on the surface, $\bar{\vec{v}}^B_A$ is the unit atmospheric velocity vector, $\bar{\vec{n}}$ is the inward-pointing unit normal of the surface, $\vec{r}_{PB}$ is the vector from the centre of mass to the centre of pressure, $A_p = \mathcal{H}\{\cos\alpha\}\cos\alpha\, A_{\text{surface}}$ is the effective projected area (with $\mathcal{H}\{\cdot\}$ the Heaviside function), and $\sigma_n$, $\sigma_t$ are the normal and tangential accommodation coefficients respectively. The total aerodynamic torque is obtained by summing \eqref{eq:aero_torque} over all exposed surfaces.

\subsection{Gravity Gradient Torque}
The gravity gradient disturbance torque \cite{wertz1999space} on a satellite is caused by the gravitational differential across a body orbiting near another large body (Earth). This force is inversely proportional to the magnitude of the orbital radius $\vec{r}_{BE}$ cubed and is thus only significant in LEO.

The gravity gradient torque can be determined by \cite{wertz1999space}:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_{GG} = \frac{3\mu}{{\vec{r}_{BE}}^3} \vec{u}_e \times (\vec{J_B}\vec{u}_e)
\end{equation}

where $\mu = 398600.5 km^3/s^2$ is the gravitational constant of Earth, $\vec{u}_e = [\vec{C}]^{OB} \begin{bmatrix}0 \ 0 \ 1\end{bmatrix}^T$ is the unit vector towards nadir in satellite body coordinates and $\vec{J}$ is the satellite's moment of inertia tensor.


\subsection{Solar Radiation Pressure Torque}
Solar radiation pressure (SRP) torque arises from the momentum transfer of incident photons striking the surfaces of the satellite. Although the pressure exerted by solar radiation is small, it acts continuously and over long durations can produce a significant secular disturbance, particularly for satellites with large surface areas such as solar panels offset from the centre of mass. Unlike aerodynamic drag and gravity gradient torques, SRP does not vanish with altitude and therefore remains relevant even outside of LEO.

The solar radiation pressure at a distance of one astronomical unit from the sun is given by \cite{wertz1999space}:
\begin{equation}
    P = \frac{S}{c}
\end{equation}
where $S \approx 1367 \ W/m^2$ is the solar constant and $c$ is the speed of light, giving $P \approx 4.51 \times 10^{-6} \ N/m^2$.

For a given flat surface, the force exerted by solar radiation pressure depends on the optical properties of the surface, namely the fractions of incident light that are absorbed, specularly reflected and diffusely reflected. The resulting force on a surface with area $A_{\text{surface}}$ and outward unit normal $\bar{\vec{n}}$ can be expressed as \cite{wertz1999space}:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{F}_{SRP} = -P A_{\text{surface}} \cos\theta_i \left[ (1 - \sigma_s)\bar{\vec{s}} + 2\left(\sigma_s \cos\theta_i + \frac{1}{3}\sigma_d\right)\bar{\vec{n}} \right]
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\vec{s}}$ is the unit vector from the satellite towards the sun, $\theta_i$ is the angle of incidence between $\bar{\vec{s}}$ and $\bar{\vec{n}}$, and $\sigma_s$ and $\sigma_d$ are the specular and diffuse reflectivity coefficients of the surface respectively, with the absorbed fraction given by $1 - \sigma_s - \sigma_d$. This force only acts on surfaces which are illuminated, i.e.\ for which $\cos\theta_i > 0$ and which are not in eclipse.

As with the aerodynamic torque, the disturbance torque about the satellite centre of mass is produced by the offset between the centre of mass and the centre of solar pressure of each surface, $\vec{r}_{PB}$, such that the total SRP torque is obtained by summing over all illuminated surfaces \cite{wertz1999space}:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_{SRP} = \sum_{k} \vec{r}_{PB,k} \times \vec{F}_{SRP,k}
    \label{eq:srp_torque}
\end{equation}

Since the direction of $\bar{\vec{s}}$ changes slowly over the course of an orbit relative to the orbital period, and reverses only when the satellite passes through the Earth's shadow, the SRP torque is typically treated as a slowly-varying or constant disturbance over the timescale of an attitude control manoeuvre.

\subsection{Magnetic Dipole Torque}
Satellites have a significant current running through their solar panels and wiring. The interaction between this current and the Earth's magnetic field produces a disturbance torque on the satellite. However with clever design this can be minimized. Solar panel manufacturers design their solar panels to mitigate this effect by using a balanced wiring layout. As such this torque is usually negligible and will be ignored in the analysis of the satellite's attitude dynamics. 

\section{Attitude Control}

\subsection{Magnetorquers}
Reaction wheels, and other momentum based control methods, have the disadvantage, when compared to other control methods, that they build up momentum over time due to external disturbance torqures leading to actuator saturation. Thus to in order to manage this momentum, the earth's magnetic field can be utilized for generating small torques in LEO. The earth's magnetic field can be approximated as a dipole field \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}. The magnetic field strength at a distance r from the center of the earth can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        B_{x} \\
        B_{y} \\
        B_{z}
    \end{bmatrix} = \frac{m}{r^3} \begin{bmatrix}
        cos(\alpha - \eta_m)sin(\xi_m) \\
        cos(\xi_m) \\
        -2sin(\alpha - \eta_m)sin(\xi_m)
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the phase angle measured from the ascending node of the orbit relative to the earth's equator $\eta_m$, $\xi_m$ and $m$ is the magnetic moment of the earth. \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997} The magnetic field can be used for attitude control by using magnetorquers which generate a torque by interacting with the earth's magnetic field. The torque generated by a magnetorquer can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_m = \vec{m} \times \vec{B}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{m}$ is the generated magnetic moment of the magnetorquer and $\vec{B}$ is the magnetic field strength at the satellite's location \cite{sidiAttitudeDynamicsKinematics1997}. Some important notes about magnetorquers are that they can only generate torque perpendicular to the magnetic field and that the magnetic field strength varies with the satellite's position in orbit. The magnetic field is strongest near the poles and weakest near the equator.  Therefore maneuvers will require careful management to ensure that the satellite can maintain control throughout its orbit after under fault condition.

\subsection{Reaction Wheel Faults}
Faults in reaction wheels can be broadly categorized as follows \cite{ZarouratiMohammad2023Daar}. Four representative fault types are defined according to how the wheel's speed characteristic deviates from its commanded value:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speed decrease:} the wheel speed remains proportional to, but scaled below, the desired value, producing a torque with high ripple. This is classified as a partial loss of effectiveness (PLOE) fault.
    \item \textbf{Deviation:} the wheel speed deviates from the desired value for some reason, producing a continuous bias torque. Since the actuator still generates a (non-zero) torque, this is classified as a hard-over failure with total loss of effectiveness (TLOE).
    \item \textbf{Stuck:} the wheel suddenly stops rotating following a step change, with bearing friction bringing the speed to zero over a characteristic time $T_z$.
    \item \textbf{Idle:} the wheel stops responding to commands altogether, and its speed remains frozen at whatever value it had at the instant the fault occurred.
\end{itemize}

Of these, only the stuck and idle faults produce no reaction torque whatsoever, and it is these two fault types that render the corresponding control axis underactuated, since all actuation authority on that axis is lost. The speed-decrease and deviation faults still produce some control torque and therefore do not by themselves create an underactuated configuration, although a speed-decrease fault can combine with a superimposed bias to form a compound loss-of-effectiveness-plus-bias fault. At the attitude control subsystem level, these actuator-level faults manifest as degraded pointing accuracy, reduced stability margins, and, for the remaining healthy wheels compensating for the fault, increased power consumption -- ultimately degrading image resolution and manoeuvrability and potentially leading to mission failure.

\section{Feedback Control}

\subsection{Quaternion Feedback Control}
The kinematics and dynamics of the attitude error, given by \eqref{eq:q_error_kinematics}, \eqref{eq:omega_error} and \eqref{eq:sat_dynamics}, can be stabilised using a nonlinear proportional-derivative (PD) quaternion feedback law \cite{wieSpaceVehicleDynamics1998}. Denoting the net torque commanded from the reaction wheels as $\vec{\tau}_c \triangleq -\dot{\vec{h}}_w$, the control law is chosen as

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tau}_c = \vec{\omega}^\times(\vec{J}\vec{\omega} - \vec{h}_w) + \vec{J}\left([\vec{C}]^{BR}\vec{\dot{\omega}}_d - \vec{\omega}_e \times [\vec{C}]^{BR}\vec{\omega}_d\right) - k_p \vec{q}_{ve} - k_d \vec{\omega}_e
    \label{eq:quat_feedback_control}
\end{equation}

where $k_p, k_d > 0$ are the proportional and derivative gains respectively, and $\vec{q}_{ve}$ is the vector part of the error quaternion $\vec{q}_e$. The first two terms cancel the gyroscopic coupling term in \eqref{eq:sat_dynamics} and feed forward the desired angular acceleration and rate, while the remaining two terms drive the attitude and rate errors towards zero. Substituting \eqref{eq:quat_feedback_control} into \eqref{eq:sat_dynamics}, and neglecting the external disturbance torque $\vec{\tau}_d$ and magnetorquer torque $\vec{\tau}_{mag}$, the angular velocity error dynamics of \eqref{eq:omega_error} reduce to the linear closed-loop form

\begin{equation}
    \vec{J}\vec{\dot{\omega}}_e = -k_p \vec{q}_{ve} - k_d \vec{\omega}_e
    \label{eq:quat_feedback_closed_loop}
\end{equation}

Stability of the equilibrium $(\vec{q}_{ve}, \vec{\omega}_e) = (\vec{0}, \vec{0})$ can be shown using the Lyapunov function candidate \cite{wieSpaceVehicleDynamics1998}

\begin{equation}
    V = k_p(1 - q_{ew}) + \frac{1}{2}\vec{\omega}_e^T \vec{J} \vec{\omega}_e
\end{equation}

which is positive definite about the desired equilibrium since $q_{ew} \leq 1$ for a unit quaternion. Differentiating $V$ along the trajectories of \eqref{eq:q_error_kinematics} and \eqref{eq:quat_feedback_closed_loop} gives $\dot{V} = -k_d \vec{\omega}_e^T\vec{\omega}_e \leq 0$, so that by LaSalle's invariance principle the attitude and rate errors converge asymptotically to zero for any positive gains $k_p$ and $k_d$. A well known caveat of this control law is the double cover of $SO(3)$ by the unit quaternions: since $\vec{q}_e$ and $-\vec{q}_e$ represent the same physical attitude, a large initial error with $q_{ew} < 0$ can drive the controller the "long way" around the eigenaxis before converging, a behaviour commonly referred to as unwinding.

The control law \eqref{eq:quat_feedback_control} implicitly assumes that the reaction wheels can supply an arbitrary commanded torque about all three body axes. This can be mitigated by keeping the system within the linear/almost linear control regime which is ensured by proper wheel sizing for desired slew rates. As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:underactuated}, once a reaction wheel axis fails such that $h_{wz} = \dot{h}_{wz} = 0$, the spacecraft becomes underactuated and \eqref{eq:quat_feedback_control} can no longer be applied directly, since it requires independent control authority on all three axes. This motivates the fault-tolerant control approaches considered in the remainder of this thesis.
To illustrate this, consider the following example. A 3 wheel satellite is commanded to track a reference quaternion trajectory using the quaternion feedback control law \eqref{eq:quat_feedback_control}. The satellite is initially at rest with an attitude error of $q_{ew} = 0.7071$ and $\vec{q}_{ve} = [0.7071, 0, 0]^T$, corresponding to a $90^\circ$ rotation about the x-axis. The proportional and derivative gains are set to $k_p = 0.1$ and $k_d = 0.01$. The resulting attitude tracking performance is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:quat_feedback_nominal}. The satellite successfully tracks the reference trajectory with small attitude errors.
